* Preface

This document explores the rich history of Public Key
Infrastructure (PKI), cryptography, and the hardware
security modules that protect our digital
communications today. Understanding this history helps
us appreciate why the tools we use in wjmca—Yubikeys,
OpenSSL, X.509 certificates—exist the way they do.

The narrative weaves together:
- The mathematical breakthroughs in cryptography
- The geopolitical tensions (especially the Cold War)
  that drove secure communications
- The evolution of hardware security
- How these elements converge in modern PKI

* The Cryptography Crisis: Before Public Key

** The "Unbreakable" Cipher Problem

For centuries, the fundamental challenge of
cryptography was deceptively simple: *how do two
parties share a secret key over an insecure channel?*

It's a problem that seems unsolvable. Imagine Alice
wants to send a secret message to Bob across enemy
territory. She has a cipher (a secret code). But to
use it, Bob needs to know the key. How can she send
him the key without an enemy (Eve) intercepting it
and reading all future messages?

During World War II, this problem played out in the
most consequential way imaginable. The Allies broke
Nazi Germany's Enigma machine—but not because the
cipher itself was weak. The Enigma was, in principle,
a clever machine. The problem was the *key*.

German operators had to change the Enigma's settings
every day. Those daily key settings had to be
distributed to every radio operator across the German
military—thousands of people, thousands of
opportunities for interception. The Allies captured
some Enigma machines and, crucially, captured some
of these key distribution lists. Each captured list
revealed patterns. Each pattern led to more messages
being broken. The codebreakers at Bletchley Park
understood: *the cipher is only as strong as the key
distribution*.

After WWII, during the Cold War, this problem became
even more acute. Military commanders needed to
communicate securely across continents. Nuclear
missiles needed launch codes. Diplomats needed to
negotiate in secret. But how could you distribute keys
to thousands of military bases without Eve
intercepting them?

The U.S. government's solution: *physical couriers*.
Armed guards carried key material in sealed briefcases
to military installations. Expensive. Slow. Logistically
nightmarish. But seemingly the only secure option.

Organizations like the NSA invested *billions* in
symmetric key cryptography (DES, later AES) because
these ciphers could be efficiently implemented in
hardware and offered practical security once you had
the key. But the fundamental problem remained
unsolved: *how do you securely distribute the keys in
the first place?*

This was the cryptographic crisis of the Cold War.
For twenty years, the best solution humanity had was
men in briefcases.

** The Symmetric Encryption Era (1950s-1970s)

- **DES (Data Encryption Standard)**, adopted 1977:
  56-bit key, enough for the 1970s but increasingly
  vulnerable
- **Cold War context**: The U.S. military used secure
  telephone networks (RED/BLACK systems) where:
  - BLACK phones sent unencrypted traffic
  - RED phones sent encrypted traffic over dedicated
    lines
  - Crypto devices (like the NSA's TEMPEST-hardened
    equipment) sat between the phone and the network

See Electrospaces.net for fascinating details on Cold
War secure communications infrastructure.

* The Revolution: Public Key Cryptography (1970s)

** The Impossible Problem Solved

In 1976, something revolutionary happened. Whitfield
Diffie and Martin Hellman published a paper titled
"New Directions in Cryptography," and in those pages,
they proposed something that cryptographers had
thought was mathematically *impossible*.

The question they asked was almost philosophical:
*Could two parties, communicating only over an
insecure channel, agree on a shared secret that no
eavesdropper could discover?*

For centuries, cryptographers would have said no.
Intuitively, it seems impossible. If Alice and Bob
exchange information over an open channel, and Eve
listens to everything they say, how can they arrange
a secret?

But Diffie and Hellman showed it was possible.

Here's the magic: Imagine Alice and Bob each pick a
secret number (known only to themselves). They each
perform a mathematical operation on their secret and
broadcast the result (anyone, including Eve, can see
it). Then, through an elegant mathematical property,
Alice and Bob can both compute the *same shared
secret*—but Eve, who only saw the intermediate
results, cannot.

It's like Alice and Bob each have a lock (public) and
a key (private). They send each other locked boxes.
When Alice receives Bob's locked box, she opens it
with her private key. Inside is a combination. Using
that combination *and* her private key, she can open
a final vault. Bob does the same with Alice's box.
Miraculously, they both end up with the same vault
contents—a shared secret. Eve, who can see all the
boxes but has no private keys, cannot open the vault.

The security rested on what cryptographers call the
"discrete logarithm problem"—in the language of
modular arithmetic, it's easy to multiply numbers,
but computationally infeasible to "un-multiply" them.
In other words, computing the shared secret is easy
if you have the private key. Computing it from public
information alone is, for all practical purposes,
impossible.

**Why this mattered**: For the first time in history,
two strangers could arrange a secret communication
over an insecure channel without any pre-arranged
keys, without couriers, without intermediaries.

The implications were staggering. Governments
realized this immediately. Communications that once
required briefcases full of physical key material
could now be secured with mathematics alone.

** The Secret Inventors: GCHQ's Ellis, Cocks, and Williamson

Here's a secret that wasn't revealed until 1997: the
British had already invented public key cryptography
before Diffie, Hellman, Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman.

The story begins at GCHQ (Government Communications
Headquarters), Britain's signals intelligence agency,
buried in the Cheltenham countryside. In the late
1960s, a mathematician named James Ellis had a
theoretical insight: was it possible to have
encryption without pre-shared keys? Could you
publish part of a cipher without compromising the
whole system?

Ellis called it "non-secret encryption"—a name that
sounds almost paradoxical. How can encryption be
non-secret? But the idea haunted him. In 1969, he
published an internal GCHQ memo outlining the
theoretical possibility. No one outside GCHQ saw it.

Then, in 1973—*three years before* Diffie and Hellman
would publish their famous paper—a young GCHQ
mathematician named Clifford Cocks had an insight.
Cocks realized that RSA (or rather, a system
mathematically equivalent to what Rivest, Shamir, and
Adleman would invent four years later) actually *worked*.
It wasn't just theory. It was practical cryptography.

A year later, in 1974, Malcolm Williamson developed
the key exchange method—the Diffie-Hellman equivalent—
entirely within the classified world.

**The Great Secrecy:**

All three inventions—Ellis' theoretical framework,
Cocks' RSA implementation, Williamson's key exchange—
were immediately classified as British state secrets.
They would remain secret for *24 years*.

Why such extreme secrecy? The answer reveals how
governments think about cryptography. Officials at GCHQ
and the British government understood what public key
cryptography meant:

1. **It would democratize encryption.** Any two
   people, anywhere in the world, could exchange
   secret messages without any infrastructure. Without
   key distribution centers. Without couriers.

2. **It threatened signals intelligence.** For decades,
   governments had intercepted communications,
   distributed keys through controlled channels, and
   broken codes. Public key cryptography threatened to
   take that power away.

3. **It was a strategic advantage.** If the British
   had public key cryptography and kept it secret,
   they could read encrypted communications that
   everyone else thought were secure. The advantage
   would be enormous.

So Britain *buried it*. While the public cryptography
world developed Diffie-Hellman (1976) and RSA (1977),
thinking they were witnessing the birth of public key
cryptography, the British knew better. They had the
real thing, locked away.

**The Public Rediscovery:**

Unaware of the GCHQ work, Whitfield Diffie and Martin
Hellman published their key exchange in 1976. One year
later, Ronald Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman
published RSA. The cryptographic community hailed
these as revolutionary.

But in a vault at GCHQ, the actual originals sat
gathering dust.

**The Declassification:**

It wasn't until December 1997—24 years later—that
GCHQ finally released the Ellis, Cocks, and Williamson
papers. The cryptographic world was astonished. The
leading science magazine *Scientific American* called
it one of the biggest stories in cryptography history.

The British had kept the secret so well that even
Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman—the men who received
prizes and recognition for inventing RSA—didn't know
that Clifford Cocks had beaten them by four years.

**The Lesson:**

This reveals something profound about how
cryptography develops. For every innovation that
becomes public—every paper, every conference, every
celebrated mathematician—there is a parallel,
classified history. Governments develop cryptography
in secret. Sometimes they keep it secret for decades.

This is why Bamford's *The Puzzle Palace* and other
histories of the NSA matter. Much of cryptography's
real story remains classified. What we know as the
"history of cryptography" is really just the *public*
history. The complete story—the secret innovations,
the covert advantage—will only be known once those
governments choose to reveal it, if ever.

For wjmca and modern PKI, this means something
important: trust algorithms that have withstood public
scrutiny, but understand that governments may have
advantages (and weaknesses) that aren't publicly known.

** RSA: The Public Implementation (1977)

One year after Diffie-Hellman (and unknown to the
academic world, *four years after* Cocks' classified
work), Ronald Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman
published RSA:
- A public-key cryptosystem that could both
  *encrypt* and *digitally sign*
- Security based on the difficulty of factoring large
  numbers

With RSA:
- Alice publishes a public key (product of two large
  primes)
- Bob encrypts a message using Alice's public key
- Only Alice (who knows the primes) can decrypt

**Digital Signatures** (the innovation for PKI):
- Alice can sign a message with her *private* key
- Anyone with Alice's *public* key can verify the
  signature
- The signature proves Alice created the message
  (non-repudiation)

This was the birth of *public* PKI. But the classified
world had already solved these problems. A practical
system required solving new problems.

** The Missing Piece: Authentication

With RSA, Alice could publish her public key, and Bob
could encrypt messages to her. But how did Bob know
the public key was *really* from Alice?

What if Eve (an adversary) published a fake key
claiming to be Alice? Bob would encrypt to the fake
key, and Eve could decrypt.

This was the "Public Key Authentication Problem."

* The Infrastructure: Building Trust (1980s-1990s)

** X.509 Certificates: Structure and Encoding

In 1988, the ITU (International Telecommunications
Union) published X.509, a standard for digital
certificates. But here's where it gets technical:
X.509 is not a file format. It's a *specification* of
what data should go in a certificate.

*** The Conceptual Structure

A certificate binds:
- A **subject's name** (using Distinguished Names:
  CN, O, ST, C, etc.)
- Their **public key** (the actual cryptographic
  material: RSA modulus + exponent, or ECC curve point)
- The **issuer's digital signature** (proves authenticity)
- **Validity dates** (notBefore, notAfter)
- **Extensions** (key usage constraints, alternative
  names, etc.)

Example (conceptually):
#+begin_src
Certificate {
  Version: v3
  SerialNumber: 1001
  Signature Algorithm: sha256WithRSAEncryption
  Issuer: CN=Root CA, O=Organization, C=SE
  Subject: CN=Alice, O=Organization, C=SE
  NotBefore: 2026-05-25 00:00:00 UTC
  NotAfter: 2027-05-25 23:59:59 UTC
  SubjectPublicKeyInfo {
    Algorithm: rsaEncryption
    PublicKey: (RSA key: modulus + exponent)
  }
  Extensions {
    KeyUsage: digitalSignature, keyEncipherment
    BasicConstraints: CA=false
  }
  SignatureValue: (hash of above, encrypted with
                   issuer's private key)
}
#+end_src

*** ASN.1: The Language

X.509 is defined in **ASN.1** (Abstract Syntax
Notation One), a language for describing data
structures. ASN.1 is *abstract*—it says "here's
the structure" but not "here's how to encode it."

Example ASN.1 definition (simplified):
#+begin_src asn1
Certificate ::= SEQUENCE {
  tbsCertificate       TBSCertificate,
  signatureAlgorithm   AlgorithmIdentifier,
  signature            BIT STRING
}

TBSCertificate ::= SEQUENCE {
  version         [0] Version DEFAULT v1,
  serialNumber    CertificateSerialNumber,
  signature       AlgorithmIdentifier,
  issuer          Name,
  validity        Validity,
  subject         Name,
  subjectPublicKeyInfo SubjectPublicKeyInfo
}
#+end_src

The beauty (and complexity) of ASN.1 is that it's
*language-agnostic*. It describes the structure
independent of how it's encoded.

*** DER: Binary Encoding

**DER** (Distinguished Encoding Rules) is how X.509
certificates are *actually encoded*. It's a **binary
format**.

When you run:
#+begin_src bash
openssl genrsa -out key.pem 4096
openssl req -new -key key.pem -out cert.csr
openssl x509 -req -in cert.csr -signkey key.pem \
  -out cert.der -outform DER
#+end_src

That `cert.der` file is a binary file. If you open it
in a text editor, you'll see garbage (binary data).

DER encoding is:
- **Deterministic**: Every ASN.1 structure maps to
  exactly one binary representation
- **Compact**: No wasted space
- **Unambiguous**: The binary format uniquely
  identifies the structure

Example: The RSA public key inside a certificate is
encoded as:
- Tag (0x30 = SEQUENCE)
- Length (number of bytes following)
- Content (RSA modulus, then exponent, both as
  integers with tags and lengths)
- Each integer: tag (0x02), length, bytes

*** PEM: Text Encoding

**PEM** (Privacy Enhanced Mail) is a *text-based*
format that wraps DER. It's what you typically see:

#+begin_src
-----BEGIN CERTIFICATE-----
MIIDXTCCAkWgAwIBAgIJAJC1/iNAZwqDMA0GCSqGSIb3
DQEBBQUAMEUxCzAJBgNVBAYTAkFVMRMwEQYDVQQIDApT
b21lLVN0YXRlMSEwHwYDVQQKDBhJbnRlcm5ldCBXaWRn
aXRzIFB0eSBMdGQwHhcNMTMwOTA0MjM1MjE3WhcNMTQw
OTA0MjM1MjE3WjBFMQswCQYDVQQGEwJBVTETMBEGA1UE
-----END CERTIFICATE-----
#+end_src

PEM is DER converted to base64 and wrapped with
header/footer. It's *text*, so you can view it in a
text editor, email it, paste it in documentation.

To convert:
#+begin_src bash
# DER to PEM
openssl x509 -inform DER -in cert.der \
  -out cert.pem

# PEM to DER
openssl x509 -inform PEM -in cert.pem \
  -outform DER -out cert.der
#+end_src

*** Why This Matters

In **wjmca**, when we store certificates:
- `root-ca/certs/root-ca.crt` is typically **PEM format**
  (text-based, human-readable, easy to inspect)
- OpenSSL defaults to PEM for `.crt` files
- Browsers and systems understand both DER and PEM
- Internally, OpenSSL parses these into memory
  structures, but storage is DER-in-PEM

When you run:
#+begin_src bash
openssl x509 -in cert.pem -text -noout
#+end_src

You're asking OpenSSL to:
1. Parse the PEM file (base64 decode, remove headers)
2. Parse the DER (binary) into an X.509 structure
3. Print it in human-readable text form

*** The Key Insight

X.509 is **abstract** (defined in ASN.1). DER is
**binary** (the wire format). PEM is **text**
(base64-encoded DER for human readability).

For cryptographic purposes, DER is what matters—it's
deterministic, compact, unambiguous. PEM is just a
convenience wrapper for humans and systems that
prefer text formats.

This is why trust is *delegated* through
signatures. If you trust the Root CA, and the Root CA
signed Alice's certificate (the issuer field in her
X.509 cert contains the root's name, and the
signature is valid), then you can trust Alice's
public key.

** Certificate Authorities: Trust Anchors

The solution to authentication: introduce a trusted
third party—the Certificate Authority (CA).

The CA:
1. Verifies Alice's identity (in person, with
   documents)
2. Issues a certificate binding her name to her
   public key
3. Signs the certificate with its own private key

Now, if Bob trusts the CA, he can trust Alice's
public key even if he's never met her.

This model emerged in the 1980s-1990s as:
- Government PKIs (militaries and intelligence
  agencies)
- Commercial CAs (VeriSign, Thawte, etc.)
- Corporate internal CAs (Microsoft, Apple, etc.)

** The Trust Model Problem

But this introduced a new risk: *what if the CA's
private key is compromised?*

If Eve steals the CA's key, she can forge certificates
for anyone. This led to:
- Physical security of CA operations (vaults, guards)
- Air-gapped offline systems
- Hardware security modules (HSMs) to protect keys

** Hardware Security Modules: Early Versions

By the 1990s, organizations protecting CA keys (and
other sensitive cryptographic material) used:

- **Smartcards**: Microprocessor-based cards that
  perform cryptographic operations internally
  - Key never leaves the card
  - Requires PIN to use
  - Vulnerable if stolen (brute-force attack on PIN)

- **HSMs (Hardware Security Modules)**: Dedicated
  appliances
  - Thales Luna, Gemalto, IBM 4765
  - Tamper-resistant hardware
  - Perform signing/decryption operations without
    exposing keys
  - Cost: $10,000-$100,000+
  - Used by government and large enterprises

For Cold War–era secure communications, the NSA's
approach was different: they designed custom hardware
for encryption/signing and stored key material in
physically isolated vaults, protected by armed guards.

See [[https://www.cryptomuseum.com][CryptoMuseum.com]]
for fascinating hardware from this era—CRAY
computers running crypto, NSA's KW-7 and KL-7
machines, the STU-III secure telephone line, the
Soviet Fialka, and the curious Swiss/German
"Schlüsselgerät 41" that survived as a Wehrmacht
field cipher.

** The Internet PKI is Born (1990s)

As the internet exploded, a commercial PKI emerged:
- **SSL/TLS** (Secure Sockets Layer, Transport Layer
  Security): Allows browsers to connect securely to
  websites
- **X.509 certificates**: Issued by commercial CAs
  (Verisign, GlobalSign, etc.)
- **Root stores**: Browsers ship with a list of
  trusted CAs

By 1995, HTTPS was standard. Users saw the green
padlock and trusted their connection to websites.

* The Crypto Wars of the 1990s: When Mathematics Became Munitions

** Cryptography as a Weapon

For most of the 20th century, the U.S. government
classified cryptography as a munition under the
*International Traffic in Arms Regulations* (ITAR).
This wasn't metaphor. Strong cryptographic software
was legally a *weapon*, in the same regulatory
category as tanks, missiles, and fighter jets.

In practice this meant:
- You could not export software with strong
  cryptography from the United States
- "Export" included publishing on the internet (a
  foreign national might read it)
- Violations carried penalties up to 10 years in
  prison and $1 million in fines
- Domestic versions of software could use strong
  crypto; export versions had to use weakened
  variants (typically 40-bit keys)

The rationale: signals intelligence agencies (NSA,
GCHQ) wanted to maintain their ability to read
foreign communications. Strong civilian
cryptography threatened that capability.

By the early 1990s, the policy was straining
against reality. The internet was global. Software
was global. The line between "domestic" and "export"
cryptography was becoming impossible to police.

** Phil Zimmermann and PGP

In June 1991, Phil Zimmermann—a software engineer
and anti-nuclear activist—released *PGP* (Pretty
Good Privacy). PGP was a free email encryption
program based on RSA. It was, by U.S. government
standards, a munition.

Zimmermann didn't sell PGP. He didn't directly
publish it abroad. He gave a copy to a friend who
posted it to a Usenet bulletin board, from which it
spread—within days—across the world.

In 1993, the U.S. Customs Service opened a
criminal investigation against Zimmermann for
"munitions export without a license." The
investigation lasted three years. Zimmermann faced
a possible federal prison sentence simply for
writing software. The case was finally dropped in
January 1996 without charges being filed.

Why did PGP matter so much? It gave ordinary
people—journalists, dissidents, activists, lovers—
the ability to communicate with the same
cryptographic strength as governments. For the
first time, encryption was *democratized*. The U.S.
government viewed this as a national security
threat.

** The Book Trick: When Software Became Literature

In 1995, while still under criminal investigation,
Zimmermann did something extraordinary. He took the
entire source code of PGP and *published it as a
book*: *"PGP Source Code and Internals"*, MIT
Press, 1995. Just under 900 pages of printed C
code, with line numbers, ready to be scanned and
compiled.

Why? Because of the First Amendment.

The U.S. government regulated *software* as a
munition. But *books* were protected speech. A
book could not be export-controlled. You could buy
"PGP Source Code and Internals" at any bookstore
and ship it anywhere in the world, perfectly
legally.

The book was printed in OCR-friendly typography on
high-quality paper. Outside the U.S., a group of
volunteers led by Norwegian cryptographer Ståle
Schumacher Ytteborg organized the *PGPi Scanning
Project*: they bought the book, scanned every
page, OCR'd the result, fixed the errors,
recompiled the source, and released *PGP
International*—a fully legal, non-U.S. version of
strong cryptography.

It was an act of legal jujitsu. Mathematics on a
hard drive was a weapon. The same mathematics on
paper was literature.

The technique was repeated. *Applied Cryptography*
by Bruce Schneier (1996) included extensive source
code in print form. When Schneier wanted to
distribute the code on a floppy disk, the disk
required an export license—but the book did not.

** The "Munitions" T-Shirt

In a related protest, cryptographer Adam Back
printed a three-line Perl implementation of RSA on
T-shirts:

#!/bin/perl -sp0777i<X+d*lMLa^*lN%0]dsXx++lMlN/dsM0<j]dsj
$/=unpack('H*',$_);$_=`echo 16dio\U$k"SK$/SM$n\EsN0p[lN*1
lK[d2%Sa2/d0$^Ixp"|dc`;s/\W//g;$_=pack('H*',/((..)*)$/)

Wearing the T-shirt outside the United States was,
by the letter of the law, exporting a munition.
The absurdity of the regulation became impossible
to ignore.

** Bernstein v. United States

In parallel, Daniel J. Bernstein—then a graduate
student at Berkeley—wanted to publish his
encryption algorithm *Snuffle*. The State
Department told him he needed an export license,
and that the source code itself, even on a
webpage, would constitute illegal export.

Bernstein sued the government. The case became
*Bernstein v. United States* (filed 1995). In
1999, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled, in
a landmark decision, that:

#+begin_quote
Source code is speech protected by the First
Amendment.
#+end_quote

This ruling, combined with *Junger v. Daley*
(Sixth Circuit, April 2000), effectively ended the
legal basis for ITAR cryptography restrictions on
published source code.

** The Crypto Wars End (Mostly)

In January 2000, the Clinton administration
substantially relaxed export controls on
cryptography. Crypto was moved from the State
Department's ITAR list to the Commerce
Department's EAR list, with most restrictions
eliminated.

Strong cryptography became legal to export. The
Crypto Wars, as they were called, were over.

Or were they?

The same battles return in new forms:
- *Apple v. FBI* (2016): Government demands a
  backdoor into iPhone encryption
- *Going Dark* debates: Law enforcement claims
  encryption prevents investigations
- *EARN IT Act* (U.S.), *Online Safety Bill* (UK),
  *Chat Control* (EU): Modern attempts to weaken
  or compel access to end-to-end encryption

The fundamental question of the 1990s remains
unresolved: *should ordinary citizens have access
to cryptography that governments cannot break?*

** The Legacy

PGP's descendants—*GnuPG* (GPG, the free
implementation written by Werner Koch in
Germany), signed git commits, the Signal Protocol,
age, sequoia-PGP—all owe their existence to the
legal precedents won in the 1990s. So does much of
public web cryptography.

When you sign a commit with `git commit -S`, you
are using a system that exists because Zimmermann
was willing to risk federal prison, and because a
Norwegian volunteer organized a project to scan
900 pages of code in 1995.

For *wjmca*, the lesson is that *open cryptography
infrastructure is a hard-won right, not a given*.
Building an internal CA today is trivial because
strong tools (OpenSSL, GPG, Yubikey) are legally
unrestricted. That wasn't true thirty years ago.
The `pass` utility we use for managing CA
passphrases is itself a GPG wrapper—a direct
descendant of Zimmermann's work.

* The Browser Encryption Story

** Before HTTPS: The Plain-Text Web

The early web (1991–1994) had no encryption. Every
HTTP request, every form submission, every
password traveled in clear text across the
internet. Anyone with network access at any hop
could read it.

This was tolerable when the web was mostly
academic. It became intolerable when commerce
arrived.

** Netscape Invents SSL (1994–1996)

In 1994, Netscape Communications was racing to
make the web commercially viable. The company
recognized that no one would type a credit card
number into a form that anyone could intercept.

Netscape's solution: add a cryptographic layer
underneath HTTP. They called it *SSL* (Secure
Sockets Layer).

The history was rocky:

- *SSL 1.0* (1994): Internal Netscape design,
  never released. Reviewed by external
  cryptographers and found to have serious flaws.
- *SSL 2.0* (February 1995): First public
  release, shipped in Netscape Navigator 1.1.
  Within months, cryptographers found weaknesses
  (truncation attacks, weak MAC construction).
- *SSL 3.0* (November 1996): Complete redesign
  with input from Paul Kocher. Much stronger.
  Became the foundation of every secure web
  connection for the next decade.

The IETF then took SSL and standardized it as
*TLS*:

- *TLS 1.0* (1999, RFC 2246): Essentially SSL 3.1
- *TLS 1.1* (2006): Mitigation for CBC attacks
- *TLS 1.2* (2008): Modern cipher suites, AEAD
- *TLS 1.3* (2018, RFC 8446): Complete redesign,
  removed legacy crypto, faster handshakes

By 2020, all SSL versions and TLS 1.0/1.1 were
deprecated. The padlock you see today is almost
certainly TLS 1.2 or 1.3.

** Export-Grade Crypto: The 40-bit Web

Because of ITAR (see Crypto Wars above), Netscape
had to ship two versions of every browser:

- *U.S. domestic version*: Strong 128-bit crypto
- *International export version*: Weakened 40-bit
  crypto (breakable by determined attackers
  within hours)

The export version's weakened ciphers became part
of the SSL/TLS protocol itself, identified as
"EXPORT" cipher suites. They lurked in TLS
implementations for *twenty years* after they
became legal to remove.

In 2015, two attacks demonstrated the cost:

- *FREAK* (Factoring RSA Export Keys): Tricked
  servers into using export-grade RSA keys, then
  factored them in hours on a single machine.
  Affected millions of websites.
- *Logjam*: Same idea applied to Diffie-Hellman
  key exchange. Used to attack TLS connections in
  the wild and likely exploited by state-level
  adversaries.

Both attacks worked because of decisions made
under 1990s export controls. The Crypto Wars had a
software half-life of two decades.

** The CA Business is Born

Selling certificates turned out to be
extraordinarily profitable. To use HTTPS, you
needed a certificate signed by a CA in the
browser's trust store. To get into the trust
store, you needed to pass an audit. Once you were
in, you could charge whatever the market would
bear.

- *VeriSign* (founded 1995, spun off from RSA Data
  Security) became the dominant CA. At its peak,
  certificates sold for $400–$1500/year.
- *Thawte* (Mark Shuttleworth's South African CA,
  founded 1995, acquired by VeriSign in 1999—the
  sale funded Shuttleworth's trip to space)
  followed a similar trajectory.
- Margins were estimated at over 90%. The actual
  cost of issuing a certificate—running OpenSSL,
  signing a CSR—was pennies.

For nearly two decades, the CA industry was a
profitable oligopoly of about a dozen companies
selling what was essentially a digital signature
service with a thin layer of identity
verification.

** The Failures of the CA System

The CA model had a structural problem: *any CA in
your browser's trust store can issue a certificate
for any site*. There are about 100 trusted CAs in
modern browsers. A compromise of any one of them
breaks security for everyone.

Real incidents:

- *2011: Comodo* breach. A reseller's account was
  compromised; attackers issued certs for Gmail,
  Hotmail, Skype, Yahoo. Attributed to the
  Iranian government.
- *2011: DigiNotar* (Dutch CA) was fully
  compromised. Attackers issued ~500 fraudulent
  certs, including for `*.google.com`, used to
  surveil Iranian dissidents. DigiNotar went
  bankrupt within weeks.
- *2015–2017: Symantec* (then owner of VeriSign)
  was found to have mis-issued thousands of test
  certificates, including for `google.com`. After
  repeated incidents, Google forced Symantec to
  divest its CA business; its certificates were
  eventually distrusted entirely.
- *2015: WoSign / StartCom*. Chinese CAs caught
  backdating certificates and other
  irregularities. Removed from browser trust
  stores.

Each incident demonstrated that the trust model
was fragile. Any of 100 CAs could be coerced,
breached, or simply incompetent.

** Certificate Transparency

After the DigiNotar incident, Google proposed
*Certificate Transparency* (CT), standardized in
RFC 6962 (2013). The idea is elegant:

Every certificate ever issued by a public CA must
be logged in a public, append-only log. Browsers
refuse to trust certificates that aren't in the
log. Site owners can monitor logs to detect rogue
certificates for their own domains.

This doesn't prevent a malicious CA from issuing
a bad certificate, but it guarantees the bad
certificate becomes *public knowledge* within
hours, so it can be detected and revoked.

Tools like `crt.sh` let anyone search the CT logs.
If a certificate exists for your domain that you
didn't authorize, you know within hours.

** Let's Encrypt and the Free HTTPS Revolution

In 2015, the *Internet Security Research Group*
(funded by Mozilla, EFF, Akamai, Cisco) launched
*Let's Encrypt*—a free, automated certificate
authority.

Two innovations made it work:

1. *Free*: No commercial revenue, so no friction
   to adoption.
2. *ACME protocol*: A standardized API for
   automated certificate issuance and renewal.
   Software like `certbot` could request,
   validate, install, and renew certificates with
   zero human intervention.

The HTTPS adoption curve broke vertically:
- 2014: ~30% of web traffic was HTTPS
- 2018: ~70%
- 2023: ~95%

Let's Encrypt single-handedly killed the
$1500/year cert market for ordinary domain
validation. By 2020 it was issuing ~3 million
certificates per day.

** The Padlock and the UX of Trust

Browsers needed a way to communicate "this
connection is secure" to ordinary users. The
*padlock icon* appeared in Netscape 2.0 (1995)
and became universal.

But what does the padlock actually mean? Over the
decades, browsers tried various conventions:

- *Padlock + URL* (1995–2005): "Connection is
  encrypted to *someone*"
- *Green padlock + name* (2005–2019, "EV
  certificates"): "Connection encrypted and the
  organization name has been verified by the CA"
- *Padlock only* (2019–now): "Connection is
  encrypted; identity claims are not surfaced in
  the UI"

The decline of Extended Validation certificates
is instructive. EV certs cost $200–$1000/year and
required identity verification. Browsers
displayed the organization name in green. Studies
eventually showed that users *did not notice* the
difference, and that phishers could buy EV certs
for nearly-identical company names. The visual
distinction was quietly removed.

Modern browsers go the opposite direction: they
warn loudly when HTTP is *not* encrypted, rather
than celebrating when it is. The default
expectation flipped.

** TLS 1.3 and the Future

TLS 1.3 (2018) was a structural cleanup:
- Removed RSA key exchange (only forward-secret
  algorithms: ECDHE)
- Removed CBC mode, RC4, MD5, SHA-1
- Encrypted more of the handshake (less metadata
  visible to network observers)
- Faster: 1-RTT handshakes instead of 2-RTT
- 0-RTT resumption for repeat connections

Work in progress:
- *Encrypted Client Hello (ECH)*: Hides the
  server name (SNI) from network observers,
  preventing ISP-level censorship of specific
  sites on shared hosts.
- *Post-quantum TLS*: Hybrid key exchange using
  algorithms like X25519MLKEM768, deployed by
  Google and Cloudflare from 2024. Defends
  against the threat of quantum computers
  breaking ECDH.

** What This Means for wjmca

The public web's PKI is a different world from an
internal CA, but the lessons transfer:

- *Trust is fragile*: any CA in your trust store
  can betray you. Keep your trust store small.
- *Automation enables hygiene*: short-lived
  certificates only work with automated renewal.
  Internal CAs benefit from the same.
- *Public visibility helps*: Certificate
  Transparency works for the public web. An
  internal equivalent (an audit log of all
  issuance) is the same idea at smaller scale.
- *The padlock is not enough*: end users don't
  read security UI carefully. Build systems that
  fail safely, not systems that warn users.

* The Evolution of Key Algorithms (2000s-2020s)

** RSA Dominates

RSA became the standard for decades:
- 1024-bit keys (1990s): Thought secure
- 2048-bit keys (2000s): More conservative
- 4096-bit keys (2010s): State-of-the-art

But RSA had a problem: *it was slow and used lots of
bits*.

To encrypt a message to 10,000 people required
10,000 RSA operations. For session encryption, you'd
use RSA to exchange a key, then use AES (a fast
symmetric cipher) for the bulk data.

** Elliptic Curve Cryptography

In the 1980s, Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller proposed
elliptic curve cryptography (ECC). The math is
different from RSA (curves instead of factoring), but
the same principle: easy in one direction, hard in
reverse.

Advantage: same security with much smaller keys
- 256-bit elliptic curve ≈ 2048-bit RSA in security
- Faster operations
- Smaller keys (important for certificates)

By the 2010s, ECC became standard for:
- TLS (now uses ECDHE for key exchange)
- DNSSEC
- Cryptocurrency (Bitcoin uses secp256k1)

** Ed25519: The Modern Standard

In 2011, Daniel J. Bernstein proposed EdDSA
(Edwards-curve Digital Signature Algorithm), with a
concrete instantiation called Ed25519:

Advantages:
- Secure against side-channel attacks (constant-time
  operations)
- Small keys (32 bytes / 256 bits)
- Fast signing and verification
- Simpler math (less room for implementation errors)

Ed25519 is now preferred by cryptographers for
*new* deployments. It's used in:
- Signal (private messaging)
- Modern TLS handshakes
- Secure shell (SSH) keys
- Certificate authorities (some issuing certs with
  Ed25519 keys)

In **wjmca**, we use:
- Ed25519 for intermediate CAs and end-entity certs
  (modern, preferred)
- RSA-4096 on the Yubikey (legacy compatibility)
- RSA-4096 for the root CA (Yubikey doesn't support
  Ed25519 in PIV mode yet)

* Swedish Cryptography: A Hidden Legacy (1940s-Present)

** Arne Beurling and the Legendary Cryptanalysis

While the Enigma machine is famous for being broken
by the Allies at Bletchley Park, a parallel—and
equally remarkable—cryptographic story unfolded in
neutral Sweden.

Picture this: It's 1940, and Swedish military
intelligence is intercepting German radio signals
encrypted with the Siemens T52 (Lorenz) machine. The
problem is, the Swedes have never *seen* the machine.
No Polish intelligence officers with smuggled Enigma
parts. No bombes grinding through millions of
possibilities. Just intercepted ciphertext and a
brilliant mind.

Enter Arne Beurling (1905-1986), a Swedish
mathematician of quiet genius. Over the course of
1940-1941, working alone with pencil, paper, and
thousands of encrypted German messages, Beurling did
something that seemed impossible: he *mathematically
deduced* the internal structure of the T52 machine
without ever laying eyes on it.

**How he did it:**

Imagine trying to understand a machine's internal
gears by listening to the pattern of its output. That
is essentially what Beurling did. He:
1. Analyzed the statistical properties of thousands
   of encrypted messages
2. Looked for patterns—repetitions, anomalies,
   mathematical regularities
3. Reasoned backward: "If the machine works this way,
   what would the output sound like?"
4. Tested his hypotheses against more intercepted
   traffic
5. Eventually reconstructed not just the algorithm,
   but the *physical machine* itself

By pure mathematics, Beurling determined:
- The number of rotors (wheels)
- How they stepped (advanced) with each letter
- The internal wiring connections

This is considered one of the greatest feats in
cryptanalysis history. At Bletchley Park, thousands
of people worked with industrial machinery (bombes),
stolen Enigma details, and enormous resources. Beurling
did it alone, with mathematics.

**The secrecy that followed:**

The Swedish government kept Beurling's achievement
secret for *decades* (not declassified until the
1970s). Why? Sweden was officially neutral during
WWII, and later non-aligned during the Cold War.
Revealing that they could break German codes would
compromise their neutrality. Moreover, the
*technique itself*—breaking a machine without seeing
it—was so powerful that it had to remain classified.

Beurling never became famous in his lifetime. While
the Bletchley Park codebreakers eventually became
celebrated (after declassification), Beurling's name
remained obscure. It's a reminder that the greatest
intellectual achievements sometimes happen in
silence, in service to national security, away from
public recognition.

**The deeper lesson:**

Beurling proved something profound: *mathematical
structure cannot be hidden*. If an algorithm has
weaknesses, careful analysis will find them. This is
why we trust algorithms like RSA and Ed25519—not
because they're secret, but because their
mathematical difficulty has been proven through
public scrutiny.

Conversely, a weak algorithm cannot be saved by
keeping its design secret. If you try to hide a bad
design, someone like Beurling will figure it out
anyway. This principle—that *strong math beats
secrecy*—remains true today and is why modern
cryptography is published openly.

** FRA: Sweden's Signals Intelligence Agency

The *Försvarets Radioanstalt* (Swedish Armed Forces
Radio Intelligence Agency) was established in 1941.
During and after WWII, FRA:
- Intercepted and analyzed radio communications
- Developed Swedish cryptanalysis capabilities
- Maintained intelligence gathering during the Cold
  War

Unlike many countries, Sweden maintained strict
neutrality, which meant:
- FRA could not openly ally with NATO or the Soviet
  Union
- Swedish cryptography developed independently
- Secure communications had to work with minimal
  external support

This independence forced Swedish engineers and
mathematicians to develop robust, in-house
cryptographic capabilities.

** Swedish Cold War Security Infrastructure

During the Cold War, Sweden faced a unique challenge:
surrounded by NATO countries (Norway, Denmark) and
the Soviet Union, but officially non-aligned.

Swedish security measures included:
- **SÄKERHETSPOLISEN (SÄPO)**: Swedish security
  police (counterintelligence)
- **Red/Black telephone systems**: Similar to U.S.
  military infrastructure, with encrypted RED phones
  and unencrypted BLACK phones
- **Secure communications**: Swedish military used
  encrypted radio and dedicated lines
- **Air defense**: Integrated radar and secure command
  networks (documented in Electrospaces.net)

Swedish telecom companies (like L.M. Ericsson)
developed secure telephone exchanges and
cryptographic devices for government use.

** Arne Beurling's Later Work: Information Theory

After WWII, Beurling shifted focus to pure
mathematics and information theory. He made
fundamental contributions to:
- Harmonic analysis
- Function spaces
- Later work influenced cryptographic thinking

Beurling's insights—that *information can be
quantified and manipulated mathematically*—are
foundational to modern cryptography. This bridges
Shannon's information theory (1948) with practical
cryptanalysis.

** Swedish Contributions to Modern Cryptography

Sweden has maintained a strong cryptographic
tradition:

1. **Crypto conferences**: Sweden hosted international
   cryptography conferences (EUROCRYPT, CRYPTO-hosted
   workshops)
2. **Academic cryptography**: Swedish universities
   (KTH, Lund) have strong cryptographic research
3. **Commercial security**: Swedish companies
   (Yubico!) have led in hardware security
4. **Privacy culture**: Sweden's early adoption of
   data protection and privacy laws (predating GDPR)
   reflected a culture of security and trust

** Why This Matters for wjmca

The Swedish cryptographic tradition emphasizes:
- **Mathematical rigor**: Beurling's example shows
  that cryptography must be mathematically sound
- **Independence and reliability**: Sweden's
  non-aligned position forced self-reliance; this
  principle applies to organizational PKI
- **Openness with security**: Modern Swedish PKI
  practices (and Yubico's transparency) show that
  security doesn't require secrecy
- **Long-term thinking**: Cold War-era Swedish
  security infrastructure was built to last decades;
  so should modern PKI

When we design **wjmca** with an offline root CA, we
echo Cold War Swedish principles: isolation,
reliability, and independence from external threats.

When we use Yubikey (a Swedish company!), we leverage
decades of Nordic security expertise.

  Sections on Swedish WWII codebreaking, the
  Siemens/Lorenz T52, Arne Beurling's reconstruction,
  and Hagelin's mechanical cipher line
- **Electrospaces.net**: Swedish Cold War telecom

- **"The Man Who Broke ENIGMA" articles**: Profiles

* The Yubikey: Personal Hardware Security (2007-Present)

** Before Yubikeys: Personal Crypto Hardware

In the pre-Yubikey era (1990s-2000s), securing
personal cryptographic keys was difficult:
- Smartcards existed but were hard to use (required
  card readers, drivers)
- Keys stored on laptops were vulnerable to malware
- No easy way for individuals to use PKI

Then Yubico, founded in 2007 in Sweden, released the
**YubiKey**:
- USB device with embedded smart card hardware
- Support for multiple protocols: OTP, U2F, FIDO2,
  PIV
- Simple, plug-and-play
- Affordable (~$50 vs. $1000+ for enterprise HSMs)

The Yubikey democratized hardware-based security.

** How Yubikeys Work

The Yubikey is essentially a tiny computer on a chip:
- Microprocessor (cryptographic processor)
- Secure storage (encrypted memory)
- PIN-based authentication
- Tamper detection (detects physical attacks)

When you use a Yubikey:
1. Insert it into a USB port
2. Authenticate with a PIN
3. The Yubikey performs crypto operations (signing,
   encryption)
4. The private key *never leaves* the device

This model is fundamentally more secure than storing
keys on a laptop, because:
- The key is isolated from the main OS
- Malware on your computer cannot steal the key
- Signing operations happen inside the Yubikey
  (the attacker would need the PIN)

** PIV Mode: Personal Identity Verification

The Yubikey's **PIV mode** (NIST SP 800-73) is what
we use in **wjmca**:
- Designed for government and enterprise use
- Stores X.509 certificates
- Performs digital signatures
- Supports RSA and Elliptic Curve keys

With a Yubikey in PIV mode, you can:
- Store your digital identity (certificate)
- Sign documents with your private key
- Prove your identity to systems that trust your CA

** Modern Alternatives

Today, there are other personal HSMs:
- **Nitrokey**: Open-source alternative, similar to
  Yubikey
- **SmartCard readers**: Use standard smartcards
  (more flexible but less polished UX)
- **TPM (Trusted Platform Module)**: Hardware built
  into modern laptops (less portable but always
  available)

But Yubikey remains the most popular personal HSM,
especially for developers and security professionals.

* The Offline Root CA Pattern (2000s-Present)

** The Problem: Root Key Compromise

By the 2000s, CAs understood a critical risk:
- If the Root CA's private key is compromised, the
  entire PKI collapses
- Every certificate ever issued becomes untrustworthy
- Attackers can forge identities

Major incidents:
- **2011**: DigiNotar (Dutch CA) compromised; used
  to issue fake Google certificates
- **2015**: StartCom and WoSign (CAs) security
  failures; browsers removed them from trust stores

** The Solution: Offline Root CA

The industry adopted a tiered architecture:

1. **Root CA** (offline, Yubikey-protected):
   - Never connected to the network
   - Only signs Intermediate CA certificates
   - Kept in a vault, rarely used
   - Protected by physical security and HSM

2. **Intermediate CA** (online):
   - Connected to a secure server
   - Issues end-entity certificates
   - Can be revoked and re-issued quickly
   - If compromised, only the intermediate's
     certificates are affected (not the root)

3. **End-entity certificates**: Issued by the
   Intermediate CA for servers, users, etc.

This *hierarchical trust* model is now standard in
enterprise and government PKI.

**In wjmca**, we implement exactly this model:
- `root-ca/` is kept offline (on a secure machine,
  protected by Yubikey)
- `intermediate-ca/` runs online (on a secure
  server)
- End-entity certs are issued by the intermediate

* Cold War Echoes: Why Offline Security Still Matters

** The Original "Air Gap"

During the Cold War, the most secure communications
systems were completely isolated from unsecured
networks. Examples:

- **RED/BLACK separation**: Military facilities had
  two separate phone systems:
  - RED: Encrypted, for sensitive communications
  - BLACK: Unencrypted, for routine traffic
  - Never connected to each other

- **Dedicated secure lines**: Important conversations
  happened over dedicated, physically isolated
  telephone lines (like the "Red Phone" hotline
  between Washington and Moscow)

- **Courier-delivered key material**: When new
  encryption keys were needed, armed couriers
  transported them in sealed briefcases

See [[https://electrospaces.net][Electrospaces.net]]
and [[https://www.cryptomuseum.com][CryptoMuseum.com]]
for details on secure telephones like the Siemens
T311, the U.S. STU-III, the "Red Phone"
Washington–Moscow Hotline (which was actually a
teletype, not a phone), and Soviet analogs.

** Why This Still Applies

The offline root CA is a modern descendant of Cold War
security thinking:
- **Air-gapped**: The root CA is not connected to
  networks
- **Physical security**: Access is controlled
- **HSM protection**: Keys are protected by hardware
  (like Cold War crypto devices)
- **Rare use**: Certificates are only issued when
  necessary (like the Red Phone—used rarely but
  reliably)

If the Cold War era taught us anything, it's that
network-connected systems are inherently vulnerable.
An offline root CA is a throwback to that wisdom.

* Hidden Stories: Bugs, Backdoors, and Broken Pads

The history of cryptography is also the history of
its failures—often catastrophic, sometimes darkly
funny, occasionally so audacious they read like
fiction. These stories matter to wjmca because they
reveal what protection looks like when it *fails*,
and why we make the design choices we do.

The single best public archive of this material is
[[https://www.cryptomuseum.com][CryptoMuseum.com]],
a Dutch-run encyclopedia maintained by Paul Reuvers
and Marc Simons since 1996. Most of what follows has
dedicated exhibits there with photographs, technical
documentation, and primary sources. It is worth
losing an afternoon to.

** PROJECT GUNMAN: The Typewriter Bugs (1984)

In the summer of 1984, the NSA executed *Project
GUNMAN*. On paper it was a routine equipment
upgrade: every piece of office equipment from the
U.S. Embassy in Moscow and the consulate in
Leningrad was crated up and flown back to the
United States. Replacement equipment was flown in.
Nothing extraordinary—except that GUNMAN was
secretly a search.

NSA technical security officers had received
intelligence (later attributed to a French source)
that the Soviets had compromised office equipment
at the embassy. They wanted every component
inspected.

After months of work, X-ray analysis at Fort Meade
turned up something extraordinary: *16 IBM Selectric
II and III typewriters* contained sophisticated,
miniaturized electronic bugs.

The bugs were a masterpiece of hardware espionage:

- Hidden inside the metal *comb bar* of the
  Selectric (the bar that runs across the machine
  beneath the print element).
- Used six tiny *magnetometers* to detect the
  position of the typewriter's "golf ball" as it
  tilted and rotated to strike each character.
- Encoded each keystroke as a 4-bit value.
- Buffered keystrokes and burst-transmitted them
  by RF to a nearby Soviet listening post.
- Could be remotely turned on and off by radio
  command.
- Drew power directly from the typewriter's mains.
- Were so well-machined that they appeared, on
  casual inspection, to be part of the original
  IBM design.

The bugs had been in place since approximately
*1976*. For eight years, the Soviets had read every
typed document at the U.S. Embassy—classified
cables, intelligence reports, diplomatic
correspondence. The plaintext.

The lesson is sobering: *no software security
matters if the hardware itself is compromised*.
This is the deepest justification for keeping a
root CA on hardware that has never been
network-connected, and for protecting keys in
inspectable devices like Yubikeys rather than in
software on general-purpose computers.

See [[https://www.cryptomuseum.com][CryptoMuseum.com]]
— Selectric bug exhibit with full technical
analysis and disassembly photos.

** The Thing: The Bug That Needed No Battery (1945–1952)

In August 1945, with WWII just ended, a Soviet
youth organization presented the U.S. Ambassador in
Moscow with a hand-carved wooden replica of the
*Great Seal of the United States*. It was a "gift
of friendship" between wartime allies. The
ambassador hung it on the wall behind his desk.

It stayed there for *seven years*.

Inside was a passive electronic bug designed by
*Léon Theremin*—the same Soviet engineer who
invented the eponymous musical instrument. Theremin
designed the device while imprisoned in a
*sharashka*, a Soviet labor camp for engineers
condemned to forced technical work.

The bug, known to U.S. intelligence as *"The
Thing"*, had no batteries, no active electronics,
and almost no metal parts. It consisted of a tuned
cavity and a thin metal antenna. When Soviet agents
in a nearby building aimed a radio beam of the
right frequency at the embassy wall, the cavity
re-emitted the beam modulated by sound vibrations
in the ambassador's office.

It was, decades before the term existed, *passive
RFID*. The Soviets could turn it on and off by
turning their radio beam on and off. When the beam
was off, the device was a piece of wood.
Electronically invisible.

The Thing was discovered in 1952 only by accident,
when a British radio operator monitoring an
unrelated frequency picked up American voices. It
took months more to track the signal to the Great
Seal. Theremin received the Stalin Prize for the
work. He never became famous in his lifetime for
the bug, though he is now considered the father of
covert listening devices and of passive RF
backscatter—the principle behind every modern RFID
card and contactless payment.

The lesson: *adversaries have decades to develop
techniques you have never thought of*. The Thing
predated commercial RFID by half a century.

See [[https://www.cryptomuseum.com][CryptoMuseum.com]]
— "The Thing" exhibit, with photographs, schematics,
and reconstruction notes.

** Operation Rubicon: The Backdoored Crypto Company (1970–2018)

In 1970, the CIA and West Germany's BND quietly
purchased *Crypto AG*, a Swiss company founded by
Swedish cryptographer Boris Hagelin. From that day
until 2018, the CIA and BND secretly *owned the
company that sold cryptographic machines to roughly
120 governments*.

The machines were deliberately weakened. For half a
century, the United States and West Germany read
the diplomatic and military traffic of:

- Iran (during the 1979 hostage crisis and after)
- Argentina (during the Falklands War; intelligence
  passed to Britain)
- Libya (used to attribute the 1986 Berlin
  discotheque bombing)
- Egypt, Syria, Saudi Arabia
- The Vatican
- Italy, Spain, and several NATO allies
- About 60 other countries

The operation, codenamed first *Thesaurus* and
later *Rubicon*, was one of the most successful
signals intelligence operations in history. The
target governments believed their machines were
neutral Swiss engineering—the same standard their
banks relied on. They were paying their
eavesdroppers for the privilege of being
eavesdropped.

It was not revealed until February 2020, when a
joint investigation by the *Washington Post*,
German *ZDF*, and Swiss *SRF* published the story
based on declassified internal CIA history.

Boris Hagelin himself—the Swedish founder, an
intersection point with the
[[*Arne Beurling and the Legendary Cryptanalysis][Swedish cryptography]]
tradition described earlier—was aware of at least
some of the operation. He had been cultivated by
*William Friedman* of the NSA from the 1950s
onward. The "gentleman's agreement" between Hagelin
and the United States—that fully strong machines
would be sold only to specific NATO countries—was
the seed that grew into full hidden ownership a
decade later.

The lesson: *trust in commercial cryptography is
trust in the supply chain*. Open-source software
and publicly audited algorithms (the OpenSSL and
Ed25519 we use in wjmca) are not ideological
preferences. They are the only known defense
against this exact attack.

See [[https://www.cryptomuseum.com][CryptoMuseum.com]]
— extensive coverage of Crypto AG, the Hagelin
machine family (C-36, C-38/M-209, C-52, CX-52,
H-460), and the Rubicon documents.

** VENONA: When the Unbreakable Cipher Broke (1943–1980)

The Soviet KGB and GRU used *one-time pads* for
their most sensitive communications during and
after WWII. The one-time pad is the only cipher
that is *mathematically* unbreakable—if the key is
truly random, used exactly once, and never reused,
the ciphertext reveals nothing whatsoever.

But during the chaos of wartime production, some
pages of Soviet one-time pads were *duplicated*.
The same key bytes were used for two different
messages. This is the one thing you cannot do with
a one-time pad. Two messages encrypted with the
same key can be XOR'd together to eliminate the
key entirely, leaving only plaintext-against-
plaintext—a difficult but solvable problem.

In 1943, U.S. Army cryptanalysts at *Arlington
Hall*, led later by *Meredith Gardner*, began
noticing statistical anomalies in intercepted
Soviet diplomatic cables. Slowly, painstakingly,
over years, they began to break partial messages.

The project, called *VENONA*, ran from 1943 to
1980. It exposed:

- Julius and Ethel Rosenberg as Soviet atomic spies
- Klaus Fuchs as the source of detailed atomic
  bomb design information passed to the USSR
- Donald Maclean and the rest of the Cambridge Five
- Hundreds of other Soviet agents in U.S.
  government, science, and industry

VENONA was kept secret until 1995. For 50 years,
the U.S. government chose *not to prosecute* many
of the spies VENONA had identified—because
revealing the break would compromise ongoing
intelligence. The Rosenbergs were executed in 1953;
the VENONA evidence against them was not made
public until decades later.

The lesson: *perfect mathematics requires perfect
operations*. A one-time pad reused is no longer a
one-time pad. In modern terms: an Ed25519 nonce
reused leaks the private key. A serial number
counter that resets leaks issuance patterns. The
algorithm is only as strong as the discipline
around it. This is why wjmca's `index.txt` and
`serial` files are not implementation details—
they are part of the security model.

** TEMPEST and Van Eck Phreaking

In 1943, a Bell Labs engineer noticed something
strange while testing a U.S. Army Signal Corps
encryption teleprinter called the *131-B2*. Each
keystroke produced a faint spike on a remote
oscilloscope across the laboratory—and the spike
revealed the *plaintext* being encrypted. The
encryption was mathematically perfect. The
electromagnetic side-channel was not.

This discovery launched a U.S./NATO program
codenamed *TEMPEST*—the study of compromising
electromagnetic emanations from cryptographic
equipment, and the engineering of shielding to
suppress them. For 30 years, TEMPEST was deeply
classified. Allied governments built entire
shielded rooms ("SCIFs") to defeat it. The Soviet
listening posts around U.S. embassies, including
the one at the embassy in Moscow, were
TEMPEST-style emanation collection sites.

Then in 1985, a Dutch researcher named *Wim van
Eck* published an unclassified paper showing that
the contents of any CRT computer monitor could be
reconstructed by picking up its electromagnetic
emanations from a parked van outside the building.
Van Eck used about $15 of equipment.

"Van Eck phreaking" became a cultural touchstone—a
reminder that the boundary between a secure system
and its environment is leakier than it looks.

Modern variants are extraordinary:

- *Acoustic cryptanalysis*: Researchers at the
  Weizmann Institute (Shamir, Genkin, Tromer, 2013)
  extracted 4096-bit RSA keys from a laptop by
  *listening to the sound of the CPU* with a
  parabolic microphone from across a room.
- *Power analysis*: Smartcard PINs and AES keys
  recovered by measuring power consumption with an
  oscilloscope.
- *Cache timing*: Cryptographic keys leaked across
  shared cloud hardware by measuring how long
  memory accesses take.

The lesson: *cryptography lives in physical
hardware*. The Yubikey we use to protect the root
CA is engineered with TEMPEST-style countermeasures
(constant-time operations, side-channel resistance,
shielded silicon) for exactly this reason. So is
modern OpenSSL, which goes to substantial effort to
make its RSA and ECDSA implementations
constant-time.

** The Hagelin Connection to wjmca

Boris Hagelin (1892–1983) deserves a closer look,
because he is the unexpected Swedish thread that
runs through all of these stories.

- Born in Russia to Swedish parents, educated at
  KTH in Stockholm.
- Took over *Aktiebolaget Cryptograph* in Stockholm
  in the 1920s.
- Designed the *C-36* and *C-38* mechanical cipher
  machines in the 1930s.
- His *M-209* (the U.S. Army's licensed version of
  the C-38) became the standard tactical cipher
  for American forces in WWII. Hundreds of
  thousands were produced.
- Made him a millionaire—reputedly the only person
  before the internet era to become wealthy from
  cryptography.
- Moved his company to Switzerland in 1948,
  renaming it Crypto AG.
- From the 1950s, cultivated by William Friedman
  of the NSA, leading eventually to the secret
  CIA/BND purchase of his company in 1970.

So the Swedish cryptography tradition that produced
Arne Beurling also produced Boris Hagelin: one a
mathematician of pure intellect who broke ciphers
without ever seeing the machine; the other an
engineer-entrepreneur whose machines were
themselves secretly broken for fifty years. They
are two sides of the same coin.

When we say wjmca draws on Nordic security
traditions—offline isolation, mathematical rigor,
hardware roots of trust—it is worth remembering
that Boris Hagelin is part of that tradition too,
and that his story is a warning. The Yubikey is a
Swedish device. So was the C-52. The difference is
that the Yubikey's silicon can be inspected,
audited, and its firmware reproduced. Hagelin's
machines could not.

See [[https://www.cryptomuseum.com][CryptoMuseum.com]]
— the Hagelin section is one of the most thorough
on the site, with every machine model, original
patents, and the Rubicon backstory.

** Why These Stories Matter for wjmca

These aren't war stories told for entertainment.
They map directly to the threat model behind every
operational choice in wjmca:

- *PROJECT GUNMAN* → trust hardware you can
  inspect; keep the root offline so it cannot be
  reached by network attacks; verify supply chain
  for every critical component.
- *The Thing* → assume adversaries have techniques
  you haven't imagined yet; depth in defense, not
  a single layer.
- *Operation Rubicon* → use open, auditable
  cryptographic tools (OpenSSL, GPG, Ed25519); do
  not depend on a single vendor's promise.
- *VENONA* → flawless math is undone by flawed
  process; document procedures, automate them,
  audit the audit logs; nonces and serials are
  load-bearing.
- *TEMPEST* → keep the root CA in a controlled
  physical environment; use hardware (Yubikey)
  engineered against side-channel attacks.

The principles we apply in wjmca are not
arbitrary. They are the residue of seventy years
of failures—often discovered the hard way, often
classified for decades, and now thankfully public
enough to learn from.

* Modern PKI: Trust in a Broken Internet

** The Post-Snowden Era

Edward Snowden's 2013 revelations showed that:
- Governments conducted mass surveillance
- Encrypted communications were targeted
- Certificate authorities themselves were
  compromised (by governments requesting fake
  certificates)

This led to:
- **Certificate Transparency (CT)**: Public logs of
  all issued certificates, so suspicious certs can
  be detected
- **DNSSEC**: Secure DNS to prevent tampering
- **ACME (Automated Certificate Management
  Environment)**: Let's Encrypt's protocol for
  issuing certificates automatically
- **Pinning**: Websites hardcode which CAs they
  trust, to prevent rogue CAs

** The Future: Decentralized Trust?

Some advocate moving beyond the CA model:
- **Blockchain-based PKI**: Public ledgers of
  identities (bitcoin addresses, DNS names)
- **Web of Trust**: No central CA; peers vouch for
  each other (used in PGP/GPG)
- **COSE/CBOR Object Signing and Encryption**: More
  flexible trust models for IoT

But for enterprise and government use, hierarchical
PKI (with offline root CAs) remains the standard.

* Why We Use These Tools in wjmca

** OpenSSL: The Standard Bearer

OpenSSL, originally written by Eric Young in 1998,
became the de facto standard for X.509 operations:
- Open-source (anyone can audit it)
- Implements TLS, PKI, cryptographic algorithms
- Powers most internet PKI

In **wjmca**, we use OpenSSL to:
- Generate private keys
- Create certificate signing requests
- Issue certificates
- Verify and inspect certificates

**Note**: OpenSSL had a famous vulnerability
(Heartbleed, 2014). Since then, it's been heavily
audited and improved.

** Justfiles: Automation with Accountability

Why use justfile recipes for CA operations?
- **Repeatability**: Same command every time (reduces
  errors)
- **Documentation**: Recipe comments explain what's
  happening
- **Auditability**: Logs show who ran what and when
- **Safety**: Recipes can include confirmations and
  dry-run modes

This echoes Cold War security practices: operations
are carefully scripted and documented.

** Org-Mode Documentation

Org-mode (from the Emacs text editor ecosystem) is
used for:
- **Procedural documentation**: Step-by-step guides
- **Audit trails**: Timestamped records of operations
- **Human readability**: Markdown-like syntax that's
  easy to read

This combines modern software practices with the
rigorous documentation standards of government and
military cryptography.

* Conclusion: Standing on the Shoulders of Giants

**wjmca** is not a novel invention. It combines:
- **60+ years of cryptographic research** (RSA, ECC,
  Ed25519)
- **Cold War security principles** (offline systems,
  air-gapping, physical security)
- **Enterprise PKI practices** (hierarchical CAs,
  certificate transparency)
- **Modern hardware** (Yubikeys making HSM security
  accessible)
- **Open-source software** (OpenSSL, justfile,
  org-mode)

By understanding this history, we understand:
1. **Why** we design PKI the way we do (threat models
   from decades of attacks)
2. **Why** we use hardware security (lessons from
   Cold War cryptography)
3. **Why** we keep roots offline (they're too
   valuable to risk)
4. **Why** standards matter (interoperability,
   auditability)

The principles are timeless. The tools evolve.
